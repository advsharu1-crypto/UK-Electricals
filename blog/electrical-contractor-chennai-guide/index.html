Residential vs Commercial Electrical Contractors: Understanding the Difference

The words electrician and electrical contractor are often used as if they mean the same thing. For a home they often do. For a shop, clinic or office the job can look quite different. Knowing how helps you ask the right questions.

  • By UK Electricals
  • Published 7 November 2026
  • 5 min read
The short answer

Residential electrical work is usually about safe, reliable power for a household: wiring, lighting, fans, sockets and a board that suits the home. Commercial work usually involves larger loads, more circuits, tighter schedules, more paperwork and less room for downtime. Whichever you need, ask the contractor to describe their scope in writing, and do not assume capabilities that have not been stated.

Electrician or electrical contractor?

An electrician does electrical work. An electrical contractor takes responsibility for a job, which may include planning, materials, a team of workers, scheduling and sign-off. In a small home, one person may be both. In a larger project the two roles are more distinct.

The useful question is not what the person is called but what they will do on your job, who will be on site and who is accountable for the result.

How residential and commercial work differ

ResidentialCommercial
Typical loadLights, fans, sockets, a few heavy appliancesMany more circuits, equipment, air conditioning, sometimes three-phase supply
ScaleOne household, often one or two people on siteLarger layouts, often more workers and more coordination
TimingUsually flexible around the familyOften planned around business hours, with limited downtime
PaperworkA written scope and billsMore drawings, scope documents and approvals, depending on the premises
Safety planningSafe isolation, earthing, breaker protectionThe same, plus the needs of the premises, such as customers and staff
Ongoing needsOccasional repairsRegular checks, since downtime has a cost

None of these is a rule. A big villa can look more like a small commercial job, and a small shop can be as simple as a flat.

What a homeowner usually needs

  • Safe wiring for the rooms in use and for what you plan to add.
  • A board and breakers that suit the household's load.
  • Lighting, fans and sockets where you will use them.
  • Clear communication, since you live in the space.

See our residential electrical services and wiring and rewiring pages for the kind of work homes ask about.

What a shop, clinic or office usually needs

  • Enough circuits for the equipment and lighting you plan to use.
  • Work scheduled so the business can keep running, for example in the evening.
  • Neat cable routes that can be understood by whoever works on them next.
  • A plan for faults, so that you know whom to call when something trips.

Questions to ask any electrical contractor

  • What exactly is included in this price, and what is not?
  • Who will be on site, and who is responsible for the work?
  • What materials will be used, and who supplies them?
  • How will the work be scheduled around us?
  • What licence or permit applies to this type of work?
  • What happens if a fault shows up after the job?
  • Can the scope be sent to me in writing?

Be careful with big claims

Contractors in every city advertise on trust. A claim such as "we handle projects of any size" is hard to check. Ask what they have been asked to handle that is close to your job, and judge the answer on its detail. This website does not list specific commercial projects, certifications or capacity for UK Electricals. If those matter, ask us directly, and ask any other contractor too.

For jobs that are bigger than a repair, it is reasonable to ask for an inspection or a short discussion first. Our electrical contractor services page describes how we approach enquiries.

Three examples

  • A two-bedroom flat being rewired. A residential job. The questions are about wiring, the board, earthing, sockets and keeping the home liveable during work.
  • A clinic being fitted out. A commercial job. The questions are about circuits for equipment, lighting, work outside opening hours and who coordinates with the other trades.
  • A small shop adding an AC. It sits between the two. The questions are about whether the supply and board can carry the load and where the AC point goes.

In each case, a short inspection and a written scope make the difference between a smooth job and an argument.

Premises with the public

If customers, patients or staff use the space, there may be extra safety expectations for the premises. These depend on the type of building and its use, so ask the building owner and the contractor what applies. Do not assume that a residential approach is enough for a business, and do not assume a contractor knows your premises' needs unless they have said so.

Choosing between the two

If you have a home, start with a residential electrician or contractor. If you have premises where customers or staff are present, ask whether the provider has handled that kind of space. If you are unsure which you are, describe the premises and the work, and let the contractor tell you how they would approach it.

Key takeaways

  • Residential and commercial jobs differ mainly in load, scale, scheduling and documentation.
  • A contractor manages a job, often with a team. An electrician may work alone. Ask who will be on site.
  • For businesses, ask how the work will be done around opening hours.
  • Do not assume a contractor can handle every size of job. Ask what they have been asked to do before and what is outside their scope.
  • Ask for scope, materials and payment terms in writing.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is an electrical contractor the same as an electrician?

Not always. An electrician does the work. A contractor takes responsibility for a job, which may include planning, materials and a team. For small jobs the two can be the same person.

Does a shop need a different kind of electrical work from a home?

It often involves more circuits, heavier equipment and tighter scheduling, but small shops can be simple. Describe the premises so the scope can be judged.

Can a residential electrician do commercial work?

Sometimes, depending on size and requirements. Ask the provider what they have handled that is close to your job, and ask for the scope in writing.

What should a written scope include?

The work to be done, what is excluded, who supplies materials, the schedule and how payment is staged.
